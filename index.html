I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of Pennsylvania, working in microeconomic theory. I am interested in how information shapes incentives and relationships, in particular when it is revealed over time. My job market paper studies contracting between a principal and an agent when neither knows how productive their relationship will be, and both learn it from the agent's output. My other work concerns monitoring and reputation, and repeated games on networks.

Job market paper

Contracting with Unknown Complementarity: Ratchets and Replacement Risk

Draft available soon

How do a firm and a worker contract while they are learning how productive their relationship is? I study a setting in which a principal and an agent contract over two periods under moral hazard. Neither knows how much complementarity there is between the agent and his assignment, which both learn from output. The agent earns limited liability rents, and the principal can only commit to the current period. In each period, the cheapest contract implementing strictly positive effort pays a bonus on an upper tail region of output when effort is always productive, and pays on both tails when effort is sometimes counterproductive. The agent's dynamic incentive to influence the principal's belief decomposes into an extensive retention force and an intensive wage force, measured against a manipulation-free benchmark. When the two forces oppose, a pessimistic prior induces over-experimentation and an optimistic one under-experimentation, with a single threshold between them. A pure-strategy equilibrium exists and is unique for a sufficiently convex cost of effort.

Working papers

Optimal Monitoring of AI Use with Reputation Concerns

with Mengjia Xia  ·  Paper

We study how an accuracy-seeking manager should monitor workers' use of AI when workers care about their reputation. A worker of privately known ability chooses between costly effort and a tool. The manager may monitor his choice at a cost. We characterize equilibrium technology choices and then endogenize monitoring. The optimal policy is simple: either the manager does not monitor and everyone uses the tool, or she monitors just enough to sustain separation, in which high-ability workers exert effort and low-ability workers use the tool. Mandatory disclosure is never optimal, and optimal monitoring is non-monotonic in the quality of the tool. Relative to the efficient benchmark, it can induce both over- and under-adoption. The policy has the same shape whether effort and the tool are substitutes or complements. When monitoring can depend on the outcome, the optimal audit targets success when able workers are rare and failure when they are common.

On the Irrelevance of Networks in Repeated Games

Under review  ·  Paper

When do interaction and information networks affect equilibrium outcomes in environments with many bilateral repeated games? Using a multimarket Cournot counterexample with symmetric firms and markets, I show that the ability of all pairs of opponents to sustain collusion in equilibrium depends on both which players interact and what each player observes about others' interactions. I then develop a general framework and identify a sufficient condition on stage-game payoffs, Optimality of Nash Reversion, under which network structure is irrelevant: an action profile is sustainable between all opponents if and only if it is sustainable in the corresponding two-player game.
